# 7. Communicating findings

## Why it matters

An international scouting director needs to know what the data can support and what action to take. A quality metric without a decision consequence is less useful than a small, well-supported recommendation.

## Simple explanation

Separate **observation** (what the data/check showed), **interpretation** (a plausible meaning) and **recommendation** (an action with a verification step). Missing disclosure might have several causes; don't turn a hypothesis into a fact.

Choose charts for the question. Missingness bars compare coverage; show n and a denominator. A scatterplot can show two quantitative variables only after you understand units and selection. Avoid a spend-total chart when the dataset omits unknown signings/bonuses. Undefined rates are not zero; small groups and missing-category treatment need visible notes.

Each chart should state population, observation year, quantity/units, sample size and limitation. If units are unresolved, label the reported source value accordingly or choose a quality chart that does not require financial interpretation.

Prioritize actions by decision relevance and effort: verify disputed identity/date fields, record provenance, distinguish unknown from zero, or collect missing information with a clear owner. Don't recommend a complex new platform for a narrow evidence problem.

## Baseball example (illustrative, not a finding)

“Some records lack disclosed amounts” is an observation only once measured. “Smaller deals may be less reported” is a hypothesis. “Maintain disclosure status and review source coverage before comparing organizations” is an action. A selected prospect board cannot justify “Team X spends less internationally.”

## Common mistakes

- A polished chart with no eligible denominator.
- Treating correlation as value/causality, or the board as the entire market.
- Burying the source years or unresolved unit in an appendix.
- Resume language claiming a finished tool or business impact before it exists.

## Practice exercise

Pick an invented teaching-data issue. Write one sentence each for observation, interpretation and recommendation. Give a 60-second spoken explanation to someone who doesn't use pandas. Have them repeat the allowed conclusion; revise if they infer a stronger claim.

## Before moving on

- [ ] My audience can identify the population and limits.
- [ ] Each chart has a purpose, units and sample size.
- [ ] I can separate evidence, hypotheses and actions.
- [ ] My five-minute presentation ends with prioritized next steps, not unsupported player judgments.
